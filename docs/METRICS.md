## Robustness: what happens when identifiers disappear

The `messy` profile blanks `payment_id` and `order_id` on 35% of settlement lines, which is not a hypothetical — Razorpay's recon report genuinely leaves them empty for some entities, and plenty of merchants export books carrying no gateway id at all. Tier 2 then has nothing to match on but amount, method and time.

| Metric | Standard | Identifiers missing | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Auto-match rate | 100.00% | 65.44% | -34.56% |
| **False-match rate** | 0.00% | **0.19%** | +0.19% |
| Exceptions open | 128 | 1,191 | +1,063 |

Per tier, with identifiers missing:

| Tier | Recall | Precision | Wrong links |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tier 2 — settlement line to order | 64.43% | 99.79% | 2 |
| Tier 1 — bank credit to payout | 100.00% | 100.00% | 0 |
| Tier 3 — reversal to original payment | 73.27% | 100.00% | 0 |

**Recall falls by roughly a third. Precision does not move.** That is the intended behaviour and the reason the fuzzy rules require a unique candidate: with no identifier, hundreds of small UPI orders share an amount and a minute, and the honest answer is that we do not know which is which. Those go to the exception queue. The alternative — picking the nearest and moving on — would have produced a far prettier match rate and a ledger nobody should trust.

## The loop: what is outstanding, and who owns it

Every unresolved exception is routed to the party who can actually fix it, and tracked by a key derived from *what the problem is* rather than a row id — so a case settled once does not come back on the next run.

These figures are **identified**, never recovered. Recovery happens when a bank credits the money back, which is not an event this system can observe. They are also never summed: a chargeback already lost and a fee a supplier owes back are not the same rupee.
